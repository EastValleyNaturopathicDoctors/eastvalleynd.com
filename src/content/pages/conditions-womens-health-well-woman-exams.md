<h2>Pap Smears and Cervical Cancer Screening</h2>

<p>Pap testing has played an important role in reducing cervical cancer. Screening recommendations, however, have changed as research has provided a better understanding of cervical cancer and HPV.</p>

<p>Since the introduction of the Pap smear, the incidence of cervical cancer has declined by more than 70% in the U.S. over the past 50 years.</p>

<p>Current screening recommendations are based on age, risk factors, previous screening results, and the type of HPV or cervical testing being used. For average-risk individuals, the American Cancer Society recommends beginning cervical cancer screening at age 25 and continuing through at least age 65. Primary HPV testing every five years is preferred, with other testing options available when appropriate.</p>

<p>At East Valley Naturopathic Doctors in Mesa, Arizona, we can discuss your screening history and help determine what testing may be appropriate based on your individual circumstances.</p>

<p>The pap smear testing we do at East Valley Naturopathic Doctors includes HPV typing, which allows us to specifically identify a more harmful or serious HPV strain, which is not commonly ascertained with your general pap smear. This allows us to determine a very individualized course of action if the pap smear does come back abnormal.</p>

<p>HPV is associated with cervical cancer, although only certain HPV types are considered high-risk for cervical cancer.</p>

<h2>Sexually Transmitted Infection Testing</h2>

<p>STI screening can also be an important part of a well woman exam. Testing recommendations depend on factors including age, sexual activity, partners, pregnancy status, and individual risk. The most common STIs are Chlamydia, Gonorrhea, Herpes, HPV, Trichomoniasis, and HIV (HPV testing is often done in conjunction with the Pap smear, as it can predict future risks for cervical abnormalities). The CDC recommends annual chlamydia and gonorrhea screening for sexually active women under 25 and for some women 25 and older who have increased risk.</p>

<p>Many STIs do not cause noticeable symptoms, which is one reason appropriate screening is important.</p>

<p>It is important to understand that you can be positive for Chlamydia and Gonorrhea and not have any symptoms.</p>

<p>Untreated chlamydia and other STIs can lead to complications, including pelvic inflammatory disease and fertility problems.</p>

<p>It should be understood that birth control pills, diaphragms, cervical caps, including condoms, do not protect you from getting an STI.</p>

<p>Condoms can reduce the risk of many STIs, but they do not eliminate the risk of every infection.</p>
