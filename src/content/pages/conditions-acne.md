<h2>The Naturopathic Approach to Acne</h2>

<p>The treatment of acne can be a real challenge with Western medicine struggling to find effective as well as safe protocols for teenagers and adults. Western medicine generally treats acne with a combination of topical and oral medications that target underlying causes like bacteria, excess oil, clogged pores, and inflammation. Topical treatments include benzoyl peroxide, which kills acne-causing bacteria; salicylic acid, which unclogs pores; and retinoids (like tretinoin or adapalene) that promote cell turnover and prevent pore blockage. Oral antibiotics such as doxycycline or minocycline reduce bacterial growth and inflammation and are trialed for moderate to severe acne. For hormonal acne, particularly in women, doctors may prescribe oral contraceptives or anti-androgen drugs like spironolactone to regulate hormonal fluctuations that contribute to acne. Some individuals can even be prescribed Isotretinoin (Accutane), a powerful oral medication for severe, persistent acne that works by shrinking oil glands, drastically reducing oil production. It’s highly effective but requires careful monitoring due to potential but severe side effects.</p>

<p><b>At <a href="/">East Valley Naturopathic Doctors</a>, our naturopathic treatment of acne may share some similar approaches as Western medicine but is also designed to treat the root causes without the use of toxic or damaging medications. Naturopathic therapy for acne proposes a three-pronged approach without the use of oral antibiotics that disrupt the gut microbiome or the use of dangerous medications such as Accutane. The first prong addresses the skin microbiome, the second the gut microbiome, and the third the localized skin congestion. </b></p>

<h2><b>SKIN MICROBIOME</b></h2>

<p>Healthy appearing skin and the absence of acne, eczema, and rosacea largely depend upon the health of our skin microbiome. The skin microbiome refers to the community of microorganisms, including bacteria, fungi, viruses, and mites, that live on the surface of the skin. This complex ecosystem plays a vital role in maintaining skin health and overall well-being. First and most important, the microbiome acts as a protective barrier against pathogenic microorganisms by outcompeting them for resources and producing antimicrobial substances. It modulates the skin’s immune responses, reducing the risk of inflammatory conditions and infections. It also helps to retain moisture and prevent the entry of harmful substances. In addition, some microbes produce vitamins and other compounds beneficial to skin health. </p>

<p>Although aging results in changes to the microbiome the three most common influences on the skin microbiome are diet, personal hygiene, and skincare routines. The use of soaps, lotions, and other skincare products can alter the balance of microorganisms on the skin for good or bad. And of course, poor nutrition, poor health, and stress also negatively impact the skin microbiome’s composition and function. Naturopathic medical doctors will often take advantage of safe topical products that target underlying causes like bacteria, excess oil, clogged pores, and inflammation. Topical treatments can include benzoyl peroxide, clindamycin lotion (a topical antibiotic not disruptive to the gut microbiome), which kills acne-causing bacteria; salicylic acid, which unclogs pores; and safer retinoids that promote cell turnover and prevent pore blockage. </p>

<h2><b>GUT MICROBIOME</b></h2>

<p>Although it is important to have an effective and thoughtful personal skincare and hygiene routine, creating a healthy gut microbiome may be the most important key to promoting a healthy skin microbiome. There are several steps to consider to achieve this:</p>

<h3><strong>Intestinal Detox</strong></h3>

<p><strong>The majority of inflammation arises from activity happening in the intestinal tract with fungi, yeast, parasites, and bacterial imbalances at the root of most concerns.</strong></p>

<h4><b>Candida</b></h4>

<p>The presence and overgrowth of fungi, yeast, and candida in the intestinal tract causes inflammation in the intestines as well as the skin. It contributes to an imbalance of the gut microbiome and is often present where bloating, sugar cravings, fatigue, and brain fog are also complaints. Interventions can include a strict candida diet, antifungal nutrients, and herbs, or antifungal prescriptions to lower the yeast and fungal load that is hijacking the gut microbiome. </p>

<h4><b>Parasites</b></h4>

<p>The presence and activity of parasites are often benign, but any sign of diarrhea, stomach pain, fatigue, and malabsorption of iron or other vitamins are suspicious symptoms arising from these ubiquitous bugs. Testing to discover specific worms or protozoa leads to more precise prescriptions and herbal therapy recommendations. Many patients report their skin health improving after treatment of intestinal parasites. </p>

<h4><b>Bacteria</b></h4>

<p>The intestinal tract houses good and bad bacteria. When we measure the bacteria types and families through available microbiome test kits, we sometimes find more than enough bacteria (think SIBO), or way too little, or we see elevated counts of pathogenic bacteria. The imbalance of bacteria families and types can result in increased autoimmunity and overactive immune responses all resulting in more inflammation and abundance of free radicals aging our body and our skin. Testing of the gut microbiome leads to specific recommendations to improve balance and reduce toxic load with an emphasis on customized fermented probiotic-rich foods. Adding homemade coconut yogurt or kefir, or adding kimchi or sauerkraut, will elevate the diversity and balance of the most important immune barriers in the human body.</p>
